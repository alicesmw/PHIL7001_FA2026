Data collection & ethics

Questions about the questions

Before we look at your answers: how did the way I constructed the survey decide what conclusions we might be able to draw from any model trained on this dataset?

1 · Non-obvious connections

What can two harmless answers reveal together?

  • You told us your age and the day you were born. Did you tell us your date of birth?
  • Last year’s survey asked for birth month. What happens when two datasets that each look safe are joined?
  • Height and weight together give BMI, a health measure. Did anyone agree to share that?
  • Travel time hints at where you live. How many people in this room share your section, gender, height and shoe size?
  • If a model trained on these columns learns something nobody was asked for, whose responsibility is that?

2 · Reported vs. revealed

Is the number you report the number you reveal?

  • We asked for your favourite number (one you chose to tell us) and the most random number you could think of. Was yours actually random?
  • If everyone’s “random” numbers were truly random, what would the histogram look like? What do you expect ours to look like?
  • When people report their own height and weight, which way do you think the errors lean?
  • Did you type your exact travel time, or round it to 15, 30 or 60 minutes? What does a model learn from rounded numbers?
  • The last page asked whether you lied. Is a self-reported answer about lying also worth believing?
  • When we train a model on survey answers, are we modelling people, or how people present themselves?

A reported number is what someone says. A revealed number shows up in what they do, like which “random” numbers a whole class actually picks.

3 · Precision of the answer

Is a more precise answer a more accurate one?

  • We asked for your favourite colour twice: once as RGB numbers, once in words. Which is more precise? Which is closer to what you meant?
  • RGB allows 256 × 256 × 256 ≈ 16.7 million colours. How many of them count as “blue”, and who decides where blue ends and teal begins?
  • Did your RGB values come from memory, a colour picker, or your screen’s settings? Would the same numbers look the same on another screen?
  • Last year’s answers included “blue” and “Blue”, “chatgpt” and “ChatGPT”. Are those different answers, or the same answer typed differently? (The AI model question is free text too.)
  • Every cleaning step is a decision. Who makes it, and does the model ever find out?

4 · Answer options

Did the answer options decide the conclusions before anyone answered?

  • The sleep question offered only “Morning lark” or “Night owl”. Could our data ever show that most people are neither?
  • Gender offered Male / Female / Other / Prefer not to say. Who ends up together in “Other”, and what can a model say about a group that small?
  • To a model, is “Prefer not to say” a category or missing data? What changes if we treat it one way rather than the other?
  • The season question offered four seasons. Does “four seasons” describe a Hong Kong year? What if someone wanted to reply “typhoon” season instead?
  • A classifier can only predict labels that exist in its training data. Who chose the labels, and who doesn’t fit them?

Recap

Before you train a model on any dataset, ask:

1 · Linked data

What do the columns reveal together that none reveals alone?

Age + birth day + timestamp

2 · Reported vs. revealed

Is this what people did, or what they said they did?

Favourite vs. “random” number

3 · Precision

How exact is each answer, and who cleaned it?

RGB values vs. colour words

4 · Answer options

Who chose the categories, and who doesn’t fit them?

Gender, season, chronotype
